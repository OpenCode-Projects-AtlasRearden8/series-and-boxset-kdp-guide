# Read-through that pays

**Read-through** is the percent of readers who buy book 1 and go on to
buy book 2. It is the single most valuable number in a series business,
because it multiplies: one reader brought in by marketing becomes two,
three, sometimes four sales without one extra cent of advertising.

This chapter does three things: shows where the money actually comes
from, gives you the back-matter plumbing that makes read-through
possible at all, and works the pay-back arithmetic for discounting
book 1 — the decision behind almost every series promotion.

## Two kinds of read-through

Store dashboards track units; readers track books. The useful number is
*units of book 2 bought per units of book 1 sold*, roughly equivalent:

```text
read-through book N → N+1  ≈  sales of book N+1 ÷ sales of book N
```

Two honest layers to keep separate in your head:

- **Sales read-through:** a 65% read-through means 65 of every 100
  book-1 buyers pick up book 2.
- **Kindle Unlimited page-read read-through:** KU readers pay in page
  reads, so "the same" reader of a 5-book series generates as many
  KENP pages as the books' page counts combined. A 5-book series fully
  binged is a *revenue moment* about 1,500 pages wide (chapter 8 has
  the page-read wage arithmetic).

Both layers point the same direction: the later books carry the money
you keep.

## Why read-through pays (the compounding)

Rough run for a healthy genre series — 65% onto book 2, then 75%
retention between sequels — measured per 100 book-1 sales:

| Book | Copies sold | Earnings at $4.99 (70% band, ~$3.49 each) |
|------|------------:|------------------------------------------:|
| 1    | 100         | $349 (the only row your marketing paid for) |
| 2    | 65          | $227                                      |
| 3    | 49          | $171                                      |
| 4    | 37          | $129                                      |
| Series total | 251 | **$876**                                 |

Per 100 book-1 buyers, the sequels add **151 extra sales and ~$527**
— over $500 of income one step downstream of the $350 you already
counted. In plain words: *the sequels' own sales — with no ad spend
behind them at all — add up to more than the money your marketing bought
directly.*

The estimate changes drastically with read-through quality:

| Book 1 → 2 | Book 2 → 3 → 4 | Extra copies per 100 | Extra income |
|-----------:|---------------:|---------------------:|-------------:|
| 80%        | 85%            | ~206                 | ~$719        |
| 65%        | 75%            | ~151                 | ~$527        |
| 50%        | 65%            | ~104                 | ~$363        |
| 30%        | 60%            | ~59                  | ~$206        |

(Path in each row: book 2 = first rate; books 3 and 4 = second rate
compounded. Rounded to whole books and nearest dollar.)

## The plumbing: six signposts that make read-through possible

Most "low read-through" is not a genre problem; it's a *wayfinding*
problem. A reader who finished book 1 at 2 a.m. has exactly one job
left to do — find book 2. Make that zero-effort:

1. **The last-page click.** The final page of every book links to the
   next-in-series product page ("Ready for Case Two? Tap here").
   Placed at the end of the **story**, and again one screen later in
   back matter for people who put the book down without finishing.
2. **A numbered "in this series" page at the front matter**, listing
   each book's title and store page order. It primes the binge before
   anyone is done reading, not after.
3. **The series tab.** Every book exists on the same series page
   (chapter 1), so the store's own "see the series" widget does some
   of the work — your front/back matter is the belt-and-braces.
4. **Consistent, obviously-same-series covers.** A reader should be
   able to recognize book 4's cover art style and see the series
   name at one glance. A series that changes cover art mid-stream
   throws away read-through by asking readers to *recognize* rather
   than *find* the book.
5. **A next-preorder on launch week.** If book 3 is on preorder at
   full price while book 2 launches, the moment a reader finishes
   book 2 there's a buy-button right at the finish line.
6. **Newsletter catch in the back matter.** A fun, honest "want
   more like this?" page — the sign-up list is read-through
   infrastructure for the *continuation* book, the next bundle and the
   next genre excursion alike.

## The pay-back arithmetic (or: does the 99-cent week pay?)

This is the arithmetic behind the classic promotion: dip book 1's price
to $0.99 for a week, and the marketing question is whether the extra
readers you gain pay back the discount you gave away.

Work it with round numbers.

**Setup:** book 1 is at $4.99 (70% band → $3.49 per sale), selling ~100
copies a month. During the promo week you want **6×** baseline sales —
600 units at $0.99 ≈ $0.35 each.

**The discount you gave away:** 600 promo sales minus the ~25 copies
you'd have sold anyway at full price → ~575 readers worth of
discounting, about **$1,800** of forgone margin ($3.15 × 575).

**The read-through pay-back:** of those 575 new (non-baseline) readers,
assume 65% buy book 2 at $4.99: 374 × $3.49 ≈ **$1,300**; then ~75%
between sequels: book 3 ≈ 281 × $3.49 ≈ $980; book 4 ≈ 211 × $3.49 ≈
$735. And, of the 575, maybe 5% take the bundle at $9.99 instead
of buying singles: 29 × $6.99 ≈ $200.

```text
give away:                        ~$1,800 (this month)
earn back, over the next months:  $1,300 + $980 + $735 + $200 ≈ $3,200
```

Roughly **1.7× pay-back** — good money, and it lives or dies on the
read-through holding.

Now watch the math slide with a weaker series:

- **Read-through at 40% onto book 2, 55% between sequels** (a series
  nobody binges): ~230 book-2 sales (~$803), ~127 book-3 (~$443), ~70
  book-4 (~$244), bundle ~$200 → about **$1,690 earned against $1,800
  given** — a wash. It only pays further if those readers also join
  your newsletter and buy the *next* series.
- **A niche with weak binging and one-book buyers** (much non-fiction:
  the reader buys the one book that solves their problem): the promo
  lifts only about 2× — ~200 units, ~175 new readers, ~30% read-through
  → roughly 53 + 32 downstream sales ≈ **$330 earned against $550
  given**. Non-fiction read-through is often near zero in dollar terms:
  the "discount week" then simply spends money.

The working rule that falls out:

> **A 99-cent week on book 1 is a read-through bet, not a sales bet.**
> The discount pays when: big volume lift (5×+), *and* read-through
> high enough (65%+ onto book 2), *and* books you let the readers
> actually follow on to. If any of the three is missing, don't run it
> — raise your promo prices instead with book 2 or book 3's discount,
> or run a bundle discount, or work the newsletter instead.

## The KU angle, in two lines

Inside Kindle Unlimited an enrolled book earns page reads, not
list-price royalties — so the $0.99 *dip* isn't even available from the
store side. The read-through bet runs in different currency there: free
days, binge behavior, and KENP pages at roughly $0.004 each (chapter 8
works the wage math in round numbers).

## Plumbing checklist

- [ ] Every book's final page links to the next-in-series product.
- [ ] Each book's back matter lists the whole series.
- [ ] Series covers visibly match (same art style; series name visible).
- [ ] Each book carries the same series name and number chapter-1
      requires.
- [ ] A newsletter/list catch for existing readers is in every book.
- [ ] Books 2 and 3 are live (or on preorder) before a discount week —
      never promo book 1 out into an empty series.
